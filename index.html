Layer-VQE: A Variational Quantum Approach for Combinatorial Optimization — Implementation and Comparative Analysis

Ember Spr

October 2025

Abstract

The noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era has stimulated extensive interest in hybrid quantum–classical algorithms. Among them, the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) and the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) have become central paradigms for solving combinatorial optimization problems on near-term devices. However, both face limitations in circuit depth, barren plateaus, and optimizer instability.This paper investigates the Layer-Variational Quantum Eigensolver (Layer-VQE or L-VQE), a recently proposed variant that introduces layer-wise parameter expansion to progressively enhance the expressivity of variational ansätz. We reproduce and analyze its performance relative to QAOA and standard VQE using custom simulations in Python and Qiskit through classical computer. Results demonstrate that L-VQE consistently achieves higher approximation ratios with reduced training instability, making it an effective strategy for NISQ-compatible optimization.

Keywords

Quantum combinatorial optimization, Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA), Layer-VQE, NISQ devices, hybrid quantum-classical optimization.

I. Introduction

In the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era, quantum processors typically consist of tens to hundreds of qubits that are inherently noisy and lack full-scale quantum error correction. This term, first introduced by Preskill (2018), describes the transitional phase of quantum computing—where hardware is powerful enough to perform meaningful computations but still limited by decoherence and gate errors.

Within these constraints, Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) have become a leading class of hybrid quantum-classical methods that can operate efficiently on NISQ devices. In VQAs, a parameterized quantum circuit (ansatz) is optimized by a classical algorithm to minimize a cost function, typically the expectation value ⟨H⟩ of a Hamiltonian representing the problem.

Among the most representative VQAs are the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) [1] and the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) [2]. QAOA alternates between cost and mixer unitaries derived from the target optimization problem, while VQE uses a more general ansatz to minimize the energy expectation value of a given Hamiltonian. Both algorithms require a careful trade-off between circuit expressivity and robustness to noise—a balance that becomes increasingly difficult as the number of layers grows.

As circuit depth increases, the optimization landscape of these variational methods often develops barren plateaus [3], where gradients vanish exponentially, and local minima, where optimization gets trapped in suboptimal regions. These effects significantly hinder convergence, particularly on noisy and shallow NISQ hardware.

To mitigate these issues, we adopt the Layered Variational Quantum Eigensolver (L-VQE) [4], a variant of VQE that grows the quantum circuit progressively, layer by layer. Rather than training a deep circuit from scratch, L-VQE first optimizes a shallow circuit, then incrementally adds new layers initialized close to identity operations. Each new layer inherits the well-optimized parameters from the previous stage, ensuring a smooth continuation in the optimization landscape.

Intuitively, this layer-wise training resembles the pre-training strategies in classical deep learning—where early layers learn coarse structures, and deeper layers refine them. This approach helps maintain meaningful gradients, reduce optimization instability, and avoid sudden jumps in parameter dimensionality that can lead to barren plateaus. Although each optimization stage is still local, the cumulative process effectively performs a global exploration through a sequence of well-conditioned subproblems.

In this work, we reproduce and analyze the original L-VQE experiments, comparing its performance with standard VQE and QAOA on Erdős–Rényi random graphs G(n,p). Our findings demonstrate that L-VQE not only achieves higher modularity scores but also provides a more stable and scalable optimization path suitable for NISQ-era quantum devices.

II. Background: QAOA, VQE, and L-VQE

Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA), proposed by Farhi et al. (2014), is a variational quantum algorithm for solving combinatorial optimization problems. A classical cost function \(C(z)\), where \(z \in \{0,1\}^n\), is mapped to a diagonal cost Hamiltonian \(H_C\) such that: \[\begin{equation} H_C |z\rangle = C(z) |z\rangle. \end{equation}\]

The goal is to prepare a quantum state \(|\psi\rangle\) that minimizes the expected energy: \[\begin{equation} \langle H_C \rangle = \langle \psi | H_C | \psi \rangle. \end{equation}\]

QAOA constructs the variational state through alternating applications of two unitaries: a cost unitary generated by \(H_C\) and a mixer unitary generated by \(H_M = \sum_i X_i\), where \(X_i\) is the Pauli-X operator. For a circuit depth \(p\), the QAOA ansatz is: \[\begin{equation} |\psi_p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle = \prod_{k=1}^{p} e^{-i \beta_k H_M} e^{-i \gamma_k H_C} |+\rangle^{\otimes n}, \end{equation}\] where \(|+\rangle^{\otimes n}\) is the uniform superposition of all computational basis states.

The objective function to minimize is the energy expectation: \[\begin{equation} E_p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \langle \psi_p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta}) | H_C | \psi_p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta}) \rangle. \end{equation}\] A classical optimizer updates \((\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta})\) iteratively to minimize \(E_p\). As \(p\) increases, the ansatz becomes more expressive, approaching the optimal solution at the cost of deeper and noisier circuits.

Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)

The Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) is a more general variational framework that aims to find the ground-state energy of a given Hamiltonian \(H\). A parameterized ansatz \(|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle\) is prepared using a quantum circuit \(U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) acting on the initial state \(|0\rangle^{\otimes n}\): \[\begin{equation} |\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle = U(\boldsymbol{\theta}) |0\rangle^{\otimes n}. \end{equation}\]

The expectation value of the Hamiltonian is evaluated as: \[\begin{equation} E(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \langle \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) | H | \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \rangle. \end{equation}\]

A classical optimizer adjusts \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) to minimize \(E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\), yielding an upper bound to the ground-state energy \(E_0\). VQE has been successfully applied to molecular energy estimation, quantum magnetism, and graph-based optimization.

However, as circuit depth increases, VQE often suffers from barren plateaus, where gradients vanish exponentially with the number of qubits, and from convergence to suboptimal local minima, especially on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices.

Layered Variational Quantum Eigensolver (L-VQE)

To mitigate these challenges, the Layered Variational Quantum Eigensolver (L-VQE) introduces a progressive, layer-by-layer optimization scheme. Instead of training a deep circuit from scratch, L-VQE starts from a shallow ansatz and gradually increases the number of layers. With new parameters \(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(L+1)}\) initialized near zero (corresponding to identity operations), this gradual expansion ensures smoother optimization trajectories and prevents abrupt jumps in the parameter landscape, which reduces the likelihood of barren plateaus and local minima. Empirically, L-VQE achieves higher convergence stability and better approximation accuracy with fewer optimization iterations compared to standard VQE.

III.Methodology

We chose Python because it offers a implemented scientific-computing ecosystem with needed libraries including NumPy, SciPy, NetworkX, Matplotlib etc. that we used to generate benchmark graphs, compute modularity matrices, and run classical optimizers for the hybrid outer loop. These libraries are widely adopted in the quantum-optimization.

We selected Qiskit library for three practical reasons tailored to our quantum-circuit simulations: (1) Hamiltonian-first workflow: Qiskit provides convenient primitives to represent the modularity Hamiltonian \(H\) as a sum of Pauli \(Z\) strings (SparsePauliOp), and to evaluate \(\langle H \rangle\) directly via save_expectation_value. Our formulation requires many-body \(Z\)-terms (up to 4-body for \(k{=}4\) communities), and Qiskit’s operator stack lets us build and measure these efficiently. (2) Hardware-efficient ansatz and simulation backends: Qiskit Aer gives statevector simulation (CPU/GPU), and we exploit its device options (e.g., device="GPU", single precision, cuStateVec) to accelerate sweeps over many seeds and depths. This is crucial for the grow-before-converge L-VQE schedule. (3) Optimizer availability: Qiskit and SciPy provide gradient-free optimizers (COBYLA) that are robust under sampling noise, which aligns with our finite-shots experiments. We also implemented a simple SMO-style coordinate descent to mirror the paper’s setup.

Alternatives considered.

We explored the feasibility of PennyLane and Cirq. PennyLane integrates smoothly with automatic differentiation frameworks, but our experiments rely primarily on expectation accessors and mixed backends (GPU statevector plus finite-shots counting) rather than gradient backpropagation; Qiskit’s Aer offered higher throughput for our specific circuits. Cirq is a strong alternative, especially for Google hardware mappings; however, building multi-qubit \(Z\)-term operators and measuring expectation values in bulk was more straightforward for us in Qiskit’s operator model. We also considered tensor-network simulators to scale depth on 1D layouts, but our L-VQE circuits are shallow and Aer-GPU already met our runtime needs.

IV. Results

Energy and Modularity Interpretation

In the formulation, the variational quantum algorithms minimize the expectation value of the problem Hamiltonian, \[E = \langle \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) | H | \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \rangle.\] Following the definition in Layer VQE: A Variational Approach for Combinatorial Optimization, the Hamiltonian \(H\) is constructed such that its ground-state energy corresponds to the optimal graph partition with maximum modularity \(Q\). Specifically, \(Q_{\text{found}} = -\,\langle H \rangle\), making the optimization of \(Q\) equivalent to minimizing the energy landscape. Therefore, lower energy values correspond to higher modularity and better community partitions.

The expectation value \(\langle H \rangle\) can take both positive and negative values, since the modularity matrix \(B_{ij}\) contains both positive and negative coefficients. A negative expectation value indicates that the quantum state lies in a lower-energy region, closer to the ground state, and thus corresponds to a partition with stronger community structure (higher modularity). Conversely, a positive \(\langle H \rangle\) implies a higher-energy configuration, producing weaker or worse-than-random partitions. Accordingly, larger \(Q_{\text{found}}\) values indicate better partitions, while smaller or negative \(Q_{\text{found}}\) values denote poorer community quality. The sign of \(\langle H \rangle\) is therefore physically meaningful and should not be replaced by its absolute value, as it directly encodes the direction of optimization and the quality of the resulting modular structure.

Interpretation Relative to the Random Baseline

The modularity \(Q\) quantifies how strongly the detected communities deviate from a random baseline. Specifically, the modularity matrix is defined as \[\begin{equation} B_{uv} = A_{uv} - \frac{k_u k_v}{2m}, \end{equation}\] where \(A_{uv}\) is the adjacency matrix, \(k_u\) and \(k_v\) are the node degrees, and \(m\) is the total number of edges. The second term, \(\frac{k_u k_v}{2m}\), represents the expected number of edges between nodes \(u\) and \(v\) in a configuration-model random graph that preserves the same degree distribution. Hence, \(B_{uv}\) measures how much the observed edge density deviates from that random expectation.

Positive modularity values (\(Q_{\text{found}} > 0\)) indicate that more edges fall within communities than would be expected by chance, signifying the presence of meaningful community structure. Conversely, negative modularity values (\(Q_{\text{found}} < 0\)) correspond to partitions that are less structured than a random graph, i.e., worse than the null-model baseline.

Our CSV/Excel tables report the following columns. We include precise definitions to avoid ambiguity:

  • algo: the algorithm used (VQE, QAOA,L-VQE, or intermediate L-VQE-stage).

  • \(n\): number of graph nodes.

  • \(k\) (communities): number of target communities. We encode \(k{=}4\) using \(2\) qubits per node.

  • L / L_final: circuit depth indicator. For VQE, L is the number of entangling layers; for L-VQE, L_final is the final number of grown layers.

  • shots: number of measurement shots used when estimating the expectation (0 means exact statevector expectation).

  • seed: random seed for graph initialization.

  • <H> or energy: the expectation value \(\langle H \rangle\) of the modularity Hamiltonian \(H\) at the final parameters. In our encoding, \(H\) is constructed so that maximizing modularity \(Q\) is equivalent to minimizing \(\langle H \rangle\); in particular we use \(Q \,=\, -\,\langle H \rangle\). Thus, “energy” here is a standard physics shorthand for the cost function value.

  • Q_found (or Q): the modularity value obtained from the final state, defined as \(Q\_{\mathrm{found}} \,=\, -\,\langle H \rangle\). This quantity can be positive or negative. Negative modularity is possible for partitions that are worse than the null model; these values should not be taken in absolute value.

  • Q\(^\*\): the reference optimum modularity used to compute an approximation ratio. For small \(n\), we compute \(Q^\*\) by brute force;.

L-VQE vs QAOA:

The experimental comparison between QAOA and L-VQE demonstrates a clear advantage for the Layered Variational Quantum Eigensolver across all tested configurations. For \(n = 7\) graphs, QAOA with depths \(p = 1\)–3 achieves modularity values below 0.03 and approximation ratios below 0.15, indicating weak community structure recovery. In contrast, L-VQE still with \(L = 1\)–2 consistently yields modularity between 0.11 and 0.15, with ratios around 0.68–0.75, corresponding to significantly improved partitions.

When scaling to \(n = 8\), the difference becomes more pronounced. Increasing QAOA depth from \(p = 2\) to \(p = 6\) produces only marginal gains (ratios from 0.02 to 0.11), whereas L-VQE with \(L = 3\) maintains higher modularity values (0.06–0.15) and ratios between 0.64 and 0.80.

Overall, L-VQE not only achieves higher modularity but also exhibits stronger robustness across random seeds. Its layer-growing optimization strategy provides stable convergence and better generalization, while QAOA suffers from parameter sensitivity and shallow-depth expressivity limits. The results confirm that L-VQE achieves superior energy minimization and modularity maximization with limited circuit depth, making it a more efficient and scalable variational framework for community detection tasks on NISQ devices.

This result highlights that L-VQE not only improves convergence stability but also translates circuit depth into robustness against measurement noise.

Moderate-shot regime: \(shots = 256\)

When the sampling budget increases to shots = 256, the measurement noise is substantially reduced, and both VQE and L-VQE show more stable convergence compared with the 128-shot case. However, quantitative comparison reveals that L-VQE consistently achieves higher modularity values across seeds and graph instances. For example, in the COBYLA-optimized runs, \(Q_{\mathrm{VQE}} = 0.027\) whereas \(Q_{\mathrm{L\text{-}VQE}} = 0.155\) for one configuration (graph_seed = 2, seed = 2), and \(Q_{\mathrm{VQE}} = 0.0079\) versus \(Q_{\mathrm{L\text{-}VQE}} = 0.1055\) for another (graph_seed = 2, seed = 0). Such consistent improvement indicates that the layer-wise variant not only achieves lower energy but also exhibits greater robustness to optimization noise.

Nevertheless,under the SMO optimizer, the difference in modularity between VQE and L-VQE becomes very small, and in a few instances, VQE even slightly surpasses L-VQE. This suggests that when statistical noise is low, the benefit of layer-wise training diminishes, as both methods can explore a smoother energy landscape.

High-shot regime: \(shots = 512\)

At the highest sampling budget (shots = 512), the effect of statistical noise weakens further, and Both VQE and L-VQE can further converge to high-quality community partitions. In this near-deterministic regime, their modularity values approach the half value of theoretical optimum \(Q_{\star}\) across all graph seeds. However, a consistent pattern emerges: L-VQE tends to reach comparable or slightly higher \(Q_{\mathrm{found}}\) values while maintaining smoother convergence and lower variance between runs in most cases. The performance gap is now relatively small, indicating that both algorithms are able to approximate the ground-state energy effectively when sufficient measurement precision is available. Nevertheless, the layerwise growth mechanism in L-VQE still provides a stability benefit, as it avoids the occasional fluctuations or shallow minima observed in some VQE trajectories. This observation suggests that L-VQE’s advantage lies not only in noise tolerance, but also in its scalability: the same progressive strategy that improves robustness under low-shot conditions also ensures consistent performance when circuit complexity and sampling budget increase.

V.Conclusion

This study compared the performance of three quantum variational algorithms—QAOA, VQE, and L-VQE—under different sampling regimes and noise conditions. Across systematic experiments with shots = 128, 256, and 512, a clear hierarchy in robustness and optimization performance was observed.

First, the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) generally produced the weakest results across all settings. Although it shares conceptual similarities with variational methods, its fixed-depth structure and discrete parameterization led to unstable convergence and limited expressivity. In most cases, QAOA yielded lower modularity values and higher energy expectations, failing to capture meaningful community structure in the tested graphs. This highlights a fundamental limitation of QAOA when applied to modularity-based community detection tasks: its circuit cannot adaptively refine the solution landscape as effectively as variational approaches.

Second, the standard Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) performed noticeably better than QAOA, especially when the sampling noise was moderate to low. However, VQE’s optimization trajectory exhibited significant variability—its convergence often depended heavily on initialization and random seeds. In noisy regimes (e.g., shots = 128), many VQE runs produced negative modularity values (\(Q_{\mathrm{found}} < 0\)), indicating partitions that are worse than random baselines. While VQE sometimes surpassed L-VQE in certain individual runs, its overall behavior was less stable and prone to fluctuations.

Finally, the Layerwise Variational Quantum Eigensolver (L-VQE) consistently delivered the most stable and reliable results. Even in high-noise conditions, L-VQE maintained better performance than the other two, showing relatively strong robustness and smoother convergence. As the sampling precision increased (shots = 256 and 512), its performance approached or slightly exceeded that of VQE while preserving much lower variance across runs. This demonstrates that L-VQE’s layerwise growth and parameter reuse enable it to avoid shallow minima and improve optimization continuity. In other words, L-VQE extends the VQE framework with enhanced noise tolerance, scalability, and stability.

It should be noted that the current experiments were conducted on relatively small-scale graphs (\(n = 10\)). Therefore, the full potential of L-VQE may not yet be visible. Larger and more complex instances are expected to amplify its advantages, as its hierarchical optimization strategy can better exploit multi-level correlations and stabilize training in higher-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Overall, the comparative analysis shows a clear performance ranking: \[\text{QAOA} \; < \; \text{VQE} \; < \; \text{L-VQE}.\] L-VQE decisively outperforms both QAOA and standard VQE in terms of modularity quality, optimization stability, and robustness against noise. Future work will extend these findings to larger qubit systems, investigate adaptive layer-growth strategies, and explore hardware implementations to assess real-device performance.
